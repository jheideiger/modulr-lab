1980s – 1990s

decline.

digital arrives, cheaper and more practical — modular becomes a bulky dinosaur.

text

the DX7 changes everything.

In 1983, Yamaha launched the DX7, a polyphonic digital synthesizer based on FM synthesis. Unlike analog modular systems, it fit in a single case, was programmed through menus rather than cables, and above all cost a fraction of an equivalent modular system's price. The DX7 sold over 200,000 units — an unprecedented commercial success for a synthesizer.

That same year, the MIDI standard was introduced, letting synthesizers from different brands communicate with each other and with the first personal computers. This interoperability further accelerated the adoption of compact digital synthesizers at the expense of large modular systems, which were hard to integrate into these new workflows.

sampling takes over.

At the same time, sampling technology advanced rapidly with machines like the Fairlight CMI and then Akai samplers, letting you record and replay any real sound instead of synthesizing it from scratch. For many musicians and producers, this approach felt simpler and more immediately musical than modular subtractive synthesis.

Modular, associated with a generation of instruments seen as dated, expensive and complex, fell largely out of commercial favor. Historic companies like Moog Music went through major financial troubles; some factories closed, some product lines were discontinued.

a niche practice survives.

Modular didn't disappear entirely: a handful of experimental musicians, electroacoustic composers and collectors kept using and maintaining existing systems. But for nearly two decades, modular practice stayed marginal, overshadowed by digital instruments that were more accessible, lighter, and perceived as more modern.

It would take until the mid-1990s for a new idea to quietly, then massively, revive interest in modular — in a form radically smaller and more accessible than the systems of previous decades.
