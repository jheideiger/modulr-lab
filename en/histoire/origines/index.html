→ history 1/5

late 1800s – 1930s

origins.

before the synthesizer, someone had to learn how to make sound out of electricity.

→ text

the telharmonium, a machine weighing tons.

The story begins in 1897, when American engineer Thaddeus Cahill filed the patent for the Telharmonium. This machine, which would eventually fill an entire room and weigh over 200 tons in its most advanced version, used spinning toothed wheels ("tone wheels") to generate alternating electrical currents matching precise musical frequencies.

The Telharmonium's sound was broadcast remotely over the telephone lines of the era — an early, unintentional form of audio streaming, decades before commercial radio. The project eventually failed commercially, held back by its cost and by complaints from phone companies whose lines were being saturated, but it planted a founding idea: sound can be born directly from electricity, with no string, reed, or column of air involved.

the theremin: playing without touching.

In 1920, Russian engineer Lev Termen (Léon Theremin) invented the instrument that bears his name. The principle was radically new: two antennas detect the position of the musician's hands in space, with no physical contact at all, to control the sound's pitch and volume. The Theremin became a worldwide curiosity, used in classical music as well as, later, science-fiction film scores.

This idea of controlling a sound remotely, through a gesture rather than a key or a string, already foreshadows a question modular synthesis would ask differently fifty years later: what controls what, and how?

the ondes Martenot and the Trautonium.

In 1928, French musician Maurice Martenot introduced the Ondes Martenot, an electronic instrument with a keyboard and a ribbon controller, capable of expressive glissandi impossible on a piano. It would be used by composers like Olivier Messiaen and is still taught today in some conservatories. That same year in Germany, Friedrich Trautwein developed the Trautonium, another monophonic electronic instrument that would later influence composers such as Oskar Sala.

These instruments share a founding gesture: taking sound out of traditional acoustics and handing it over to electricity. It would take another thirty years, and the invention of the transistor and control voltage, for the idea of a modular system — one where you can freely reconfigure the signal's path — to be born. That's the subject of the next stage.
