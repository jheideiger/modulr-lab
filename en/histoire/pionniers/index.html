→ history 2/5

1950s – 1960s

pioneers.

two opposing visions of modular are born almost at the same time, a few hundred miles apart.

→ text

the RCA Mark II, a synthesizer that fills a room.

In 1957, RCA installed the Mark II Sound Synthesizer at the Columbia-Princeton Electronic Music Center in New York — an imposing machine programmed using a punched paper tape rather than in real time. The composer specified every parameter in advance — pitch, timbre, duration — and the machine executed them with a precision no traditional instrument could match.

Composer Milton Babbitt became its most famous user, producing works of unprecedented rhythmic complexity. But the Mark II remained a laboratory object, one of a kind, reserved for a handful of institutions — nothing like an instrument you could own or carry around.

Robert Moog and the keyboard approach.

In the mid-1960s, engineer Robert Moog, working with composer and musician Herbert Deutsch, developed a system of interconnectable modules controlled by voltage — the principle of voltage control. His most defining decision was as much aesthetic as technical: he chose to pair his modules with a piano keyboard, making the instrument immediately familiar to a classical or jazz musician.

That choice rooted the Moog synthesizer in a recognizable instrumental tradition, which would considerably ease its adoption by musicians from other backgrounds in the following decade.

Don Buchla and the rejection of the keyboard.

At the same time, on the American west coast, engineer Don Buchla developed an equally modular system built on the opposite philosophy. Commissioned by composers Morton Subotnick and Ramon Sender for the San Francisco Tape Music Center, Buchla deliberately rejected the keyboard, which he saw as carrying too many conventional musical habits.

He preferred capacitive touch plates and step sequencers, designed to generate music that broke away from equal temperament and Western scales. From the very start, Moog and Buchla thus embodied two ways of thinking about modular: one extending the instrumental tradition, the other seeking to break free of it. That tension still runs through modular practice today.
