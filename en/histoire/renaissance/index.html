2000s – today

renaissance.

a smaller, more open format gives modular a second life — the one you're practicing today.

text

Doepfer invents a standard.

In the mid-1990s, German engineer Dieter Doepfer designed a module format radically smaller and cheaper than the historic Moog or Buchla systems: the Eurorack format. Each module fits within a width measured in "HP," runs on standardized voltage, and above all, any manufacturer can produce their own modules compatible with everyone else's.

This choice of openness, rather than a closed proprietary format, proved decisive: it let the ecosystem grow organically, without depending on a single manufacturer. It's exactly the format you've discovered throughout this course.

a worldwide community emerges.

Through the 2000s and 2010s, a growing number of independent manufacturers — Make Noise, Mutable Instruments, Intellijel, and many others — joined the Eurorack format, each bringing their own approach to sound and interface. Specialized trade shows like Superbooth in Berlin bring this new ecosystem together every year, and forums, YouTube channels and online communities let musicians worldwide trade patches and discoveries.

Unlike the systems of the 1970s, expensive and reserved for professional studios, Eurorack lets you start small — a few modules, a modest case — and grow your system gradually, module by module, at the pace of your means and your curiosity.

and software makes it all accessible.

In 2017, the software VCV Rack appeared: a free, open-source Eurorack simulator that faithfully reproduces the experience of cabling and patching directly on a computer. For the first time, discovering modular practice required no hardware investment at all.

A hundred and thirty years after the Telharmonium and its tons of toothed wheels, modular synthesis now fits inside a free piece of software, or a single case. The story isn't over: new manufacturers, new ideas and new practices keep appearing every year. You're now part of it.
