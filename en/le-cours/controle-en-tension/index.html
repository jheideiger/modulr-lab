the voltage that connects every module — one output, three destinations, a single principle.

control voltage, in one sentence.

Every module you met in the previous lesson has one or more inputs marked CV. A control voltage isn't a sound: it's a message. A value that rises and falls, which the module on the receiving end translates into action — higher, more closed, louder. The word "voltage" should be taken literally: it's an actual electrical voltage, measured in volts, running through the same kind of cable as sound.

What sets an audio signal apart from a control signal isn't its nature, then — it's its speed and its use. An audio signal oscillates hundreds or thousands of times a second, and our ear hears it as sound. A control signal changes slowly, or just once, and moves a parameter rather than producing a sound. But the line between the two is blurrier than it looks — more on that below.

one output, three destinations.

Take a single control output — an envelope, or a generic function module. Plug it first into an oscillator's 1V/octave input: the pitch follows the exact shape of that voltage, it rises when the voltage rises, it falls when the voltage falls.

Unplug that cable, without changing anything else at the source, and plug it into a filter's cutoff CV input instead: now it's the timbre that breathes at the same rhythm, while the pitch stays perfectly still. Unplug it again, plug it into an amplifier's CV input: now it's the volume that rises and falls, this time without touching either pitch or timbre.

Nothing changed at the source. The voltage sent is exactly the same in all three cases. The only thing that changes is the destination — the input the cable lands on. That's the whole principle of control voltage: the receiving module decides what the voltage means, not the module sending it.

one signal, two speeds.

There's a nuance that often surprises beginners: nothing stops you from sending a very fast control signal, or an audio signal into a control input. Plug an audio oscillator, tuned very low, into another oscillator's frequency CV input, and instead of a slow vibrato you get audible frequency modulation — the principle behind FM synthesis, which predates modular but finds total freedom within it.

What lesson 2 called harmonics can therefore, on a modular system, become a control tool in its own right. There's no fixed boundary between "what you hear" and "what controls" — only a question of speed and destination.

why this changes everything.

On a classic synthesizer, every knob controls a single parameter, wired once and for all at the factory. On a modular system, the same output can drive any CV input in the system, and that destination can change with every patch. That's what "modular" actually means: not that the modules are physically assembled together, but that the control path itself is reconfigurable, cable by cable.

This is the thread running through the whole course. Lesson 1 laid out signal, lesson 2 explained how that signal becomes timbre, lesson 3 showed the functions that produce, transform and control it. Control voltage is what connects these three building blocks — the common language that lets one module speak to another.

what now?

This course gave you the basics: signal, harmonics, the fundamental modules, and the principle that connects them all. You're not an expert — but you now have the keys to understand what's happening when you look at a patch, yours or someone else's.

Modular is an endless playground. Every cable you plug in differently creates a new configuration, a sound no one has heard before. There's no wrong patch — there are only experiments that teach you something.

If this introduction made you want to go further, the history of modular is waiting: five chapters, from the origins to the Eurorack revival. And from October, every video on the channel will have its own patch note on this site: the diagram, the wiring, and an exercise to do for real — this time with actual cables.
