what is a
sound?

understanding the raw material of modular synthesis before touching your first cable.

→ video
→ text

sound is vibration.

Before talking about synthesizers, modules or cables, let's start with something much simpler: what we hear. A sound is a vibration that travels through a medium — usually air — all the way to our ears.

When an object vibrates, it creates small variations of pressure in the air. Our ears turn these variations into sensations, which we perceive as sounds.

A synthesizer produces the same kind of variation, but electronically: an oscillator generates a signal that changes continuously over time. That signal is the raw material of everything you're going to build.

frequency: pitch.

Frequency is the number of cycles the signal completes each second. It's measured in hertz (Hz). The higher the frequency, the higher we generally perceive the sound to be.

On a picture of the signal over time, you can see these cycles: a signal that oscillates slowly has a low frequency, a signal that oscillates quickly has a high frequency.

amplitude: level.

Frequency isn't the only thing that can change. The same sound can keep exactly the same pitch and change in amplitude: what changes then is the size of the variation.

On a picture of the signal, a larger amplitude means a wider variation around its resting position. To the ear, this shows up notably as a difference in perceived loudness.

Frequency and amplitude: we already have two essential ways of describing a signal.

produce, transform, control.

This is where the synthesizer gets interesting. A signal isn't just something you listen to: you can also produce it, measure it, modify it… and even use it to control something else.

In a modular system, that's exactly what we work with. Some signals produce sound. Others transform it. Others still control the way the system evolves over time. These three functions are the subject of lesson 3, and the last one — control — has a lesson of its own, the fourth.

what's next?

Before connecting all of this, one essential question remains: why can two instruments play exactly the same note… without sounding the same? That's what we'll find out in the next lesson: fundamental, harmonics and timbre.
