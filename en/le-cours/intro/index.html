what is a
signal?

understanding the raw material of modular synthesis before touching your first cable.

→ video
→ text

sound is movement.

Before we talk about modules, cables, or voltage, there's one fundamental thing to understand: a sound is a vibration. A membrane moving, air oscillating, an electrical signal rising and falling. Everything starts there.

In a modular synthesizer, this vibration is represented by an electrical signal — a voltage that varies over time. This signal is the raw material for everything you're about to build.

audio vs. control voltage.

There are two big families of signals in a modular system: audio signals and control signals (CV, for Control Voltage).

An audio signal vibrates fast enough for your ears to perceive it as sound — typically between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz. A CV signal moves more slowly: it's used to control the parameters of other modules. For example, a CV can control a note's pitch, how open a filter is, or an amplifier's volume.

The beauty of modular is that both types of signal are technically identical — they're both just voltage. The only difference is how you decide to use them. An LFO (low frequency oscillator) is usually a control signal, but if you speed up its frequency past 20 Hz, it becomes an audio signal. This interchangeability sits at the heart of modular philosophy.

the patch: your sonic architecture.

Every cable you plug in creates a connection between two modules. The sum of these connections forms what's called a patch — literally, a "wiring." Each patch is a unique sonic architecture.

Unlike a classic synthesizer where the signal path is fixed (oscillator → filter → amplifier → output), a modular system leaves everything up to you. You can send a filter's output into another filter, use an audio signal to modulate an LFO, or have a microphone control an oscillator's pitch. There's no wrong connection — there are only experiments.

In the next lesson, we'll dive into acoustic basics: frequency, harmonics, and timbre. You'll understand what sets the sound of a guitar apart from a synthesizer — and how an oscillator builds all of that from nothing.
