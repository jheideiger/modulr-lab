sound and
harmonics.

where the timbre of things comes from — and how an oscillator builds sound out of nothing.

→ video
→ text

it all starts with a vibration.

A sound is air moving. More precisely, it's a pressure variation propagating through the air as a wave. When that wave reaches your eardrum, it makes it vibrate — and your brain interprets that vibration as sound.

The most fundamental trait of a sound is its frequency: how many times the wave oscillates per second, measured in Hertz (Hz). A frequency of 440 Hz means the air vibrates 440 times a second — that's the note A, the one orchestras tune to.

The higher the frequency, the higher-pitched the sound. The lower it is, the deeper the sound. The human ear perceives frequencies roughly between 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz.

the sine wave: the purest sound.

The simplest waveform is the sine wave. It's a perfectly smooth curve that rises and falls regularly. It contains only a single frequency — no complexity, no texture. It's the "barest" sound that exists.

In practice, a pure sine wave sounds like a soft whistle, a bit like a phone's beep. It's a sound with no character, no personality. And that's exactly why it's interesting: it serves as the basic building block for understanding every other sound.

harmonics: where character comes from.

In real life, no instrument produces a pure sine wave. A guitar, a voice, a violin — all produce a sound made up of several simultaneous frequencies. The lowest of these frequencies is called the fundamental, and all the others are harmonics.

Harmonics are multiples of the fundamental. If your fundamental is at 100 Hz, the harmonics will be at 200 Hz, 300 Hz, 400 Hz, and so on. It's the mix of these harmonics — how many there are, their relative intensity — that creates a sound's timbre. That's what lets you tell a guitar apart from a piano even when they play the same note.

the classic waveforms.

In a synthesizer, the oscillator (VCO) generates electrical waveforms that correspond to different mixes of harmonics. The four classic shapes are the sine (no harmonics), the triangle (weak odd harmonics), the sawtooth (all harmonics, very rich), and the square (strong odd harmonics, a hollow, nasal sound).

Each waveform has its own color, texture, character. And it's by combining them, filtering them, modulating them that you sculpt sound in a modular system. That's exactly what we'll look at in the next lesson: what the modules that process these signals are actually for.
