VCV Rack: modular without the budget.

VCV Rack is a free, open-source piece of software that simulates a Eurorack modular system on your computer. The interface faithfully reproduces the physical experience: modules in a rack, cables to plug, knobs to turn. It's the perfect tool for learning without spending a cent.

Download VCV Rack from vcvrack.com, install it, and launch it. You'll find yourself facing an empty rack. That's normal — in the modular world, everything starts with an empty space and choices to make.

step 1: an oscillator and an output.

The simplest patch in the world: a VCO plugged directly into the audio output. In VCV Rack, add a VCO-1 module (right-click the rack → add the Fundamental VCO-1 module) and an Audio module (Fundamental Audio). Plug the VCO's SAW output into the Audio module's input.

You should hear a continuous sound: a raw sawtooth. It's a bit harsh, but it's your first patch. You've just created a signal path: oscillator → output. Now let's make it interesting.

step 2: add a filter.

Add a VCF module (Fundamental VCF). Unplug the cable between the VCO and the Audio module, and insert the filter between them: VCO → VCF → Audio. Turn the filter's FREQ knob: you'll hear the sound open and close. That's the cutoff frequency at work.

Now, bring up the RES (resonance) knob a bit. The filter starts to whistle slightly around the cutoff frequency — that's resonance boosting the frequencies near the cutoff. That characteristic sound is the signature of subtractive synthesis.

step 3: add movement with an LFO.

The sound is static. Add an LFO-1 module (Fundamental LFO-1). Plug its TRI (triangle) output into the filter's CV input (the one labeled FREQ CV). Set the LFO's frequency fairly low — around 1 Hz.

The filter opens and closes on its own, rhythmically. The LFO sends a voltage that rises and falls slowly, and that voltage controls the filter's cutoff frequency. You've just discovered modular's core principle: a signal controlling a parameter. That's what Control Voltage means.

step 4: an envelope to play notes.

Right now, the sound runs continuously. Add an ADSR module (Fundamental ADSR) and a VCA module (Fundamental VCA). Plug: VCF → VCA → Audio. Then plug the ADSR's output into the VCA's CV input.

The ADSR needs a trigger signal (gate) to work. Add a SEQ-3 module (sequencer) or simply a gate button. Each gate pulse triggers the envelope: the sound appears, holds, and fades according to the Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release settings.

Congratulations — you have a complete patch: oscillator → filter → amplifier, controlled by an LFO and an envelope. That's the fundamental patch of subtractive synthesis, the one 90% of electronic music is built on.

what now?

This course gave you the basics: signal, harmonics, fundamental modules, and your first patch. You're not an expert — but you now have the keys to understand what you're doing, and to explore on your own.

Modular is an endless playground. Every cable you plug in differently creates a new configuration, a sound no one has heard before. There's no wrong patch — there are only experiments that teach you something.

If this introduction made you want to go further, explore VCV Rack's modules, watch patching videos, join the community. And if you have questions or feedback, feel free to write to us.
