patch note 5 · the VCO

What an oscillator does: the DPO's VCO.

A voltage that rises and falls, again and again. Slowly, you hear every pass; fast enough, it becomes a note. On a single oscillator, the DPO's VCO A, you hear the three ideas that make a VCO: speed, shape, and the voltage that controls the note.

result

what you'll get.

An oscillator you hear move from rhythm to note, change timbre without changing pitch, then play a little melody on its own, sent by a sequencer.

listening

what to listen for.

  1. From rhythm to note. Without René, slowly turn Coarse up from fully counterclockwise, on the sawtooth: separate clicks speed up, then become a note at around twenty hertz.
  2. Timbre. On the same note, switch from one output to another: the sine wave has almost no color, the triangle a little more presence, the sawtooth is bright, the square hollow. The pitch doesn't move.
  3. The voltage that plays. Patch René in: the oscillator follows every step. The sequencer makes no sound; it sends instructions.
variations

three variations.

  1. Playing by hand. Replace René with Pressure Points: each plate sends its voltage into the 1V/Oct input, and you choose the note with your fingertips.
  2. Changing shape while the sequence runs. Keep René and move cable 3 from one output to another: the melody stays the same, only its color changes.
  3. A vibrato. MATHS, channel 4 in Cycle, slow Rise and Fall, into VCO A's Expo input, with its attenuator almost closed: the note wavers gently around its pitch.
your turn

your turn to listen.

Recognizing waveforms by ear. Tune VCO A and VCO B to the same note. Close your eyes and ask someone to patch an output at random: sine, triangle, sawtooth or square. Name it before you look. On your own, patch without looking at the panel labels, listen, then check.
